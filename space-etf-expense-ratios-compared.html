What an Expense Ratio Actually Is

An expense ratio is the yearly cost of owning a fund, shown as a percent of what you have invested. A fund with a 0.50% expense ratio costs you $50 a year for every $10,000 you hold.

You never get a bill for it. The fund takes a tiny slice out of its assets every day, and the share price you see already has it removed. That's exactly why people ignore it. It's invisible. But it comes out in good years and bad years, whether the fund is up 40% or down 40%.

The SEC has a short bulletin called How Fees and Expenses Affect Your Investment Portfolio that makes the same point with its own numbers. Small differences in fees turn into big differences in what you end up with, because every dollar paid in fees is a dollar that stops compounding for you.

The Cheapest Space ETFs

Now just the non-leveraged funds, which is where most long term investors will be shopping.

$UFOX is the cheapest at 0.30%. The Defiance Space and Connective Tech ETF holds 63 stocks and has $728M in assets. One thing to know before you buy it on price alone: it's a space and connectivity fund, and its biggest holdings include large tech companies as well as space names. So you're paying less partly because you're getting a broader, less pure portfolio.

$XAR and $DIPR tie at 0.35%. SPDR S&P Aerospace & Defense ETF is a broad aerospace and defense fund with $4.75B in assets, and Corgi Space & Satellite Communications ETF is a small pure space fund with $1.2M. Same fee, very different funds. With a fund as small as $DIPR, I'd also look at how much it trades each day before buying, because a cheap fee doesn't help if the spread is wide.

At 0.50% you get the first of the big name pure space funds. $ORBX (Global X Space Tech) and $WARP (VanEck Space) both launched in 2026 at 0.50%. $ROKT is slightly cheaper at 0.45% and has been around since 2018.

0.75% is the going rate for the most popular funds. $UFO, $ARKX, $NASA and $MARS all charge 0.75%, and so do the newer $XSPC, $GALX and $WSPC. That's 7 of the 14 space funds at exactly the same price.

If you're trying to pick between funds at the same fee, the fee can't break the tie. What's inside does. I compared the four biggest new ones in $NASA vs $MARS vs $ORBX vs $WARP and the three oldest in $UFO vs $ARKX vs $ROKT.

Aerospace and defense funds are the cheapest group. $XAR at 0.35% and $ITA at 0.37% are huge, simple index funds from the largest issuers, and scale brings costs down. $ITA has $11.3B in assets. Spreading fixed costs over that much money is a lot easier than spreading them over $5 million.

Space funds cost more, and I think that's fair up to a point. They're smaller, many are actively managed, and several now hold SpaceX, which took real work to own before it went public. Weighted by assets, the average dollar in a space fund pays about 0.68%.

Hypothetical: $10,000 invested once, growing 7% a year before fees, with the fee taken each year. "Fees" is the gap between the ending value with no fee and the ending value with the fee, so it includes the growth you lose on money paid out. Real returns will be very different.

At 0.30%, fees cost about $2,257 over 20 years. At 0.75%, the most common price for a space fund, it's about $5,409. That's a gap of roughly $3,152 on a single $10,000 investment, for funds that own a lot of the same companies.

Two things I'd take from that table.

The difference between 0.50% and 0.75% is real but not huge. It's about $1,718 over 20 years on $10,000. If the 0.75% fund holds what you actually want and the 0.50% fund doesn't, I wouldn't let a quarter point make the decision.

The difference between 0.30% and 1.50% is enormous. About $7,838. That's why I'd never hold a leveraged fund for years even if the daily reset didn't exist.

When Is a Higher Fee Worth It?

I don't think cheapest always wins. If it did, everyone would own the same fund. Here's how I think about paying more.

Pay for what you can't get elsewhere. If a fund gives you something specific, like a heavy SpaceX weight or a true pure play portfolio with no big tech filler, that can be worth an extra 0.25%. You can see exactly who owns how much SpaceX in my list of ETFs that hold SpaceX.

Don't pay extra for the same holdings. Before you buy the pricier fund, pull up the top ten holdings of both. If eight of the ten match, you're paying more for a different logo.

Size and age count for something. A fund with $2 million in assets can close. When that happens you get your money back at net asset value, but it can trigger taxes at a bad time. Several funds on my list are under $5 million right now. A slightly higher fee on a fund that's clearly going to survive can be the better deal.

Past returns don't justify a fee. A fund that beat its peers last year isn't more likely to do it again because it charges more. I ranked the whole field by return in Best Performing Space ETFs in 2026, and the best performer over the past year was one of the cheaper funds.

Costs the Expense Ratio Doesn't Show

The expense ratio is the main cost, but it's not the only one. Three more to keep in mind.

The bid/ask spread. Every time you buy or sell, you pay the gap between what buyers are offering and what sellers are asking. On a huge fund like $ITA that gap is usually a penny. On a tiny, thinly traded fund it can be much wider. If you trade often, this can cost more than the expense ratio. Using a limit order helps.

Premiums and discounts. An ETF's price can drift a little above or below the value of what it holds, especially in small funds on wild days. It usually corrects, but it's worth a glance at the issuer's site before a big purchase.

Taxes. Most space funds pay little in dividends, so there's not much tax drag while you hold. The income funds are different. Their large payouts are taxable in a regular account, and how they're taxed depends on what the payout is made of.

None of these show up in the fee table. All of them come out of your return.

My Take

I've spent years building a portfolio that pays me, and the one habit that's helped the most is boring: I look at costs first. I do it with dividend funds at TopDividendETFs.com, and I do it here.

For space, my honest read is that 0.50% to 0.75% is a reasonable price for a focused fund in a young, fast moving industry. I wouldn't pay 0.99% for a plain stock fund when similar ones cost half that. And I'd treat anything above 1% as a trading tool, not an investment.

The good news is that fees have been coming down as more issuers pile in. Two years ago you had a handful of choices. Now there are 14 non-leveraged space funds fighting for your money, and several of the 2026 launches came in at 0.50% or less. Competition is doing what it's supposed to do.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the cheapest space ETF?

Of the funds I track, the cheapest non-leveraged space fund is the Defiance Space and Connective Tech ETF ($UFOX) at 0.30%, followed by $DIPR at 0.35% and $ROKT at 0.45%, based on StockAnalysis.com data checked November 22, 2026.

What is a good expense ratio for a space ETF?

Most pure space ETFs charge between 0.50% and 0.75%, and the average across the 14 non-leveraged space funds I track is 0.66%. Broad aerospace and defense index funds are cheaper, averaging 0.57%.

What are the expense ratios of UFO, ARKX, NASA and ROKT?

$UFO charges 0.75%, $ARKX 0.75%, $NASA 0.75% and $ROKT 0.45%, according to StockAnalysis.com fund pages checked November 22, 2026.

Why are leveraged space ETFs so expensive?

They use swaps and options to deliver 2x or -2x the daily move of a stock, which costs more to run than holding shares. The leveraged and inverse funds I track charge between 0.75% and 2.91%, and financing costs inside the swaps come on top of that.

How much does a 0.75% expense ratio cost?

About $75 a year for every $10,000 invested. In a hypothetical where $10,000 grows 7% a year before fees, a 0.75% fee adds up to roughly $1,427 over 10 years and $5,409 over 20 years, including the growth lost on the fees paid.

Do I pay the expense ratio directly?

No. The fund deducts it from its assets a little each day, so it is already reflected in the share price and the returns you see. You will not get a bill for it.

What You're Actually Buying With Kratos

Kratos is based in San Diego, has about 4,300 employees and reports two segments. Here's what each brought in during the third quarter of 2026 (the quarter ended September 27, 2026):

  • Kratos Government Solutions, $397.2 million (up 52.5%, 35.8% organic): the big engine. This includes its space and satellite business (virtualized ground systems that command, track and control satellites), microwave electronics, turbine engines, defense rocket systems and hypersonics, plus training and cyber. The fastest organic growth was in Defense Rocket Systems at 149.0%. Space, Training and Cyber grew 11.6% organically.
  • Kratos Unmanned Systems, $74.6 million (down from $87.2 million): jet powered drones like the Valkyrie and target drones. Kratos says last year's number included a $16.1 million Valkyrie shipment to an international customer.

The space piece matters for a site like this one. Kratos says its Space and Satellite business has a significant new opportunity pipeline, and that its bid and proposal pipeline rose to $15.5 billion, with the most notable increases in space, training and hypersonics. On November 12, 2026 it also announced a portable terminal for space domain awareness under its Knownspace line.

Here's how I read it. The business numbers are strong: revenue up 35.7%, a record $2.65 billion backlog, more than twice as many new orders as revenue last quarter, and a 2027 forecast that calls for roughly 18% to 22% more growth. But the stock is down about 62% in a year anyway. That's the gap you have to be comfortable with. The company is still burning cash as it builds factories and inventory for drones, engines and hypersonics, it barely breaks even on a GAAP basis, and the share count is up about 13% in a year.

The Risks I'd Think About

  • Volatility: a 52-week range of $26.60 to $134.00. It fell 9.31% on November 20, 2026 alone.
  • Government funding: almost all of its revenue depends on US and allied defense budgets. Kratos itself says US defense funding is running under an extended Continuing Resolution, and it expects Q1 2027 to be its lightest quarter because of it.
  • Cash burn: free cash flow was negative $29.4 million in the quarter, and Kratos expects heavy spending on production capacity to keep weighing on cash flow.
  • Thin profits: a GAAP net loss of $0.2 million on $471.8 million of revenue. Adjusted numbers look better, but they leave out stock compensation and amortization.
  • Dilution: shares outstanding rose about 13.4% over the past year.
  • Fund concentration: even in $ARKX, Kratos is about 5%. If SpaceX, Rocket Lab or Tesla fall, that will move these funds a lot more than Kratos will.

Does Kratos Pay a Dividend?

No. Kratos doesn't pay a dividend as of November 2026, and none of the four ETFs above is built for income. My Take

I'm a dividend investor first. I reached financial freedom by building income, not by guessing which defense contractor wins the next drone program. But Kratos is a good example of why I always look inside a fund before buying it. Most people would assume a "space ETF" owns the satellite ground system makers. For Kratos, the real exposure is in two ARK funds and a defense ETF.

The company's orders and backlog look like a business that's growing fast. The stock looks like one investors have lost patience with. Both can be true for a while. If you own $ARKX or $ARKQ, you already own about 5% Kratos, so I wouldn't stack a big direct position on top of that without knowing it. And keep any single stock like this to a size you can hold through another 50% drop, because it just did that.
