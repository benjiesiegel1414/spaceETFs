Do Space ETFs Pay Dividends? Yields for Every Space Fund in 2026

Only 10 of the 33 space and aerospace ETFs I track paid a dividend this year. The regular funds all yield under 1%. Two income funds list 17% and 51%. Here's every payer, the dollars per share, and what the big yields really returned.

From Our Network
Quick answer: A few do, but not much. Of the 33 space and aerospace ETFs I track, only 10 paid a dividend in the past year. The traditional funds all yield under 1%: $SHLD is the highest at 0.77%, then $UFOX (0.56%), $PPA (0.45%) and $UFO (0.42%). Two options income funds pay far more, $SPCI (weekly, 51% listed yield) and $XSHP (monthly, 17%), but both have lost money overall since launch, even counting every payout.

I'm a dividend guy. I built my income one payout at a time, and it's the first thing I look for in any fund. So when I started tracking space ETFs, the question I kept coming back to was simple: do any of these things actually pay you?

The honest answer is "barely," with two big exceptions that need a closer look. I went through all 33 funds on my space ETF rankings, pulled the yield, the dollars per share and the payout schedule for each, and then dug into the two high yield funds to see what investors really ended up with.

Two very different groups are hiding in that table. The top two rows are options income funds built to throw off cash. Why Most Space ETFs Pay Almost Nothing

An ETF can only pass along what it collects. A regular stock ETF collects dividends from the companies it owns, takes out its fee, and sends you the rest. So the yield of a space ETF is really just the yield of space companies, minus the expense ratio.

And space companies mostly don't pay dividends. SpaceX doesn't. Rocket Lab doesn't. AST SpaceMobile, Planet Labs, Intuitive Machines, Firefly: none of them. These are young companies spending every dollar they have, and plenty they've borrowed, on rockets, satellites and factories. A lot of them aren't profitable yet. Paying shareholders would mean building slower, and nobody who owns them wants that.

The dividends that do show up come from the older names that sit inside these funds: the big defense contractors, satellite operators with steady contracts, and industrial companies with a space division. The more of those a fund owns, the more it yields. That's why the aerospace and defense funds have some yield and the pure "new space" funds launched in 2026 have none.

There's a fee effect too. If a fund's holdings yield 0.8% and the fund charges 0.75%, there's almost nothing left to hand you. I laid out every fund's fee in Space ETF Fees Compared, and you'll notice the funds with the lowest fees tend to be the ones that manage to pay something.

I'll be straight with you: nobody should buy any of these for the income. The dividend is a small bonus on top of whatever the share price does. If the fund drops 10%, a 0.4% dividend isn't going to make you feel better.

The Income Funds: $SPCI and $XSHP

Now the two that look like they belong on one of my dividend sites.

$SPCI, the Tuttle Capital Space Industry Income Blast ETF, pays every week. It holds a basket of space stocks and runs an options strategy on top to bring in cash, then sends that cash out. It lists a yield of about 51%.

$XSHP, the Kurv SpaceX Enhanced Income ETF, pays monthly. It's built around SpaceX exposure with an options overlay, and it lists a yield of about 17%.

Those are eye catching numbers, and I own plenty of options income funds myself, so I'm not here to tell you the whole category is bad. But a yield that size always comes with a trade. When a fund sells options on volatile stocks, it collects a lot of premium, and that's where the big payout comes from. In return it gives up some of the upside when those stocks jump, while still taking most of the hit when they fall. On top of that, part of a payout like this can be a return of your own capital instead of income the fund earned. The fund tells you the breakdown in notices on its website, and it's worth reading them.

Here's what happened to the weekly payout on $SPCI since it launched.

It started at $0.20 a share each week, rose to $0.30 through June and early July, and has stepped down since. The most recent weekly payout was $0.07. That's not a fund cutting its dividend the way a company would. Payouts on funds like this tend to follow the share price and the option income available, so when the share price falls the payout usually shrinks along with it. The yield percentage can stay sky high while the dollars you actually receive keep dropping.

Yield vs Total Return: The Part That Matters

This is the single most important idea in income investing, and it applies to every high yield fund, not just these two. Yield tells you what came out. Total return tells you what you have. Total return is the price change plus every payout added back in.

Take $SPCI. Its share price fell from $24.94 to $12.27, a drop of 51%. Along the way it paid out about $6.25 a share across 35 weekly payouts. Add those back and the total return is -38.0%. So the payouts softened the loss a lot. They did not turn it into a gain.

$XSHP tells the same story on a smaller scale. Price down 35%, $2.70 a share paid over 5 months, total return -24.5%. For comparison, SpaceX stock itself was -33.4% over those same dates. So $XSHP did lose less than the stock it's built around, which is roughly what an options income fund is supposed to do in a falling market.

The fair way to read this. Both funds launched into a terrible stretch for space stocks. Nearly every space fund that started in 2026 is down 20% to 40%. So these two aren't unusually bad. The point is that a 50% yield did not protect anyone from a falling market, and nobody should expect it to.

If you like this style of fund, I track dozens of them with payout history and price decay on WeeklyETFs.com and MonthlyETFs.com. Whatever fund you're looking at, I'd always check total return since launch before the yield.

Space ETFs That Pay No Dividend

The other 23 funds I track show no dividend yield at all. That includes the biggest pure space names: $NASA, $ARKX, $MARS, $ORBX and $WARP. It also includes $XOVR, $JEDI, the small 2026 launches like $GALX, $DIPR, $WSPC, $XSPC and $UFOD, and every leveraged and inverse fund.

A few of the new ones may make a small year end payout in December, since funds have to distribute any net income and gains they've collected. I wouldn't count on it being meaningful. A fund that's been open six months and holds mostly companies that pay no dividends doesn't have much to distribute.

The leveraged funds are their own case. They hold swaps and cash, not dividend paying stocks, so there's usually nothing to pass along. More on how those work in Leveraged SpaceX ETFs Explained.

How I'd Get Income and Space in the Same Portfolio

I think the cleanest answer is to stop asking one fund to do both jobs.

Let space be the growth piece. Pick a regular space fund for what it owns and what it costs, accept that it will pay you little or nothing, and size it so a 40% drop wouldn't change your life. I ranked them by return in Best Performing Space ETFs in 2026, and there's a beginner walkthrough in Space ETFs 101.

Let income funds be the income piece. Funds that are built to pay, and that hold companies with real cash flow, do that job far better than a space fund ever will. That's the whole reason I run TopDividendETFs.com.

If you want a little of both in one ticker, the aerospace and defense funds are the closest thing. They own profitable companies, pay a small quarterly dividend and still give you some space exposure through the big contractors. You won't get rich on a 0.3% yield, but over the long run those funds have been some of the best performers on my whole list.

And if you're tempted by the 50% yield, go in knowing what it is: a way to turn a volatile theme into cash flow, with the share price likely to wear down over time. Keep it small, and judge it on total return.

My Take

Space is the one corner of the market where I've made peace with not getting paid. The companies that will matter most over the next decade are still in building mode, and I'd rather they spend the money on rockets than send me a check.

So I keep the two things separate. My income comes from funds designed for income. My space exposure is a small, long term bet that I don't need anything from today. When someone asks me which space ETF pays the best dividend, my real answer is that it's the wrong question. Ask which one you'd be happy to hold for ten years with no dividend at all, and then get your income somewhere else.

Is there a monthly dividend space ETF?

Is the SPCI yield safe?

I would not think of it as a fixed yield. $SPCI's weekly payout has ranged from $0.30 to $0.07 per share since launch and its share price is down about 51%, for a total return of -38.0% including payouts through November 20, 2026.

What You're Actually Buying With Kratos

Kratos is based in San Diego, has about 4,300 employees and reports two segments. Here's what each brought in during the third quarter of 2026 (the quarter ended September 27, 2026):

  • Kratos Government Solutions, $397.2 million (up 52.5%, 35.8% organic): the big engine. This includes its space and satellite business (virtualized ground systems that command, track and control satellites), microwave electronics, turbine engines, defense rocket systems and hypersonics, plus training and cyber. The fastest organic growth was in Defense Rocket Systems at 149.0%. Space, Training and Cyber grew 11.6% organically.
  • Kratos Unmanned Systems, $74.6 million (down from $87.2 million): jet powered drones like the Valkyrie and target drones. Kratos says last year's number included a $16.1 million Valkyrie shipment to an international customer.

The space piece matters for a site like this one. Kratos says its Space and Satellite business has a significant new opportunity pipeline, and that its bid and proposal pipeline rose to $15.5 billion, with the most notable increases in space, training and hypersonics. On November 12, 2026 it also announced a portable terminal for space domain awareness under its Knownspace line.

Here's how I read it. The business numbers are strong: revenue up 35.7%, a record $2.65 billion backlog, more than twice as many new orders as revenue last quarter, and a 2027 forecast that calls for roughly 18% to 22% more growth. But the stock is down about 62% in a year anyway. That's the gap you have to be comfortable with. The company is still burning cash as it builds factories and inventory for drones, engines and hypersonics, it barely breaks even on a GAAP basis, and the share count is up about 13% in a year.

The Risks I'd Think About

  • Volatility: a 52-week range of $26.60 to $134.00. It fell 9.31% on November 20, 2026 alone.
  • Government funding: almost all of its revenue depends on US and allied defense budgets. Kratos itself says US defense funding is running under an extended Continuing Resolution, and it expects Q1 2027 to be its lightest quarter because of it.
  • Cash burn: free cash flow was negative $29.4 million in the quarter, and Kratos expects heavy spending on production capacity to keep weighing on cash flow.
  • Thin profits: a GAAP net loss of $0.2 million on $471.8 million of revenue. Adjusted numbers look better, but they leave out stock compensation and amortization.
  • Dilution: shares outstanding rose about 13.4% over the past year.
  • Fund concentration: even in $ARKX, Kratos is about 5%. If SpaceX, Rocket Lab or Tesla fall, that will move these funds a lot more than Kratos will.

Does Kratos Pay a Dividend?

No. Kratos doesn't pay a dividend as of November 2026, and none of the four ETFs above is built for income. That's the trade-off with the whole space and defense tech theme: you're betting on growth and accepting big swings. Which Approach Fits You

  • You want the most Kratos in one fund: $ARKX at 5.16%, with SpaceX and Rocket Lab as the anchors.
  • You want Kratos inside a robotics and autonomy fund: $ARKQ at 4.85%, where it's the third biggest holding.
  • You want the lowest fee and broad defense exposure: $XAR at 2.05%, with a 0.35% expense ratio.
  • You want the defense giants with a little Kratos on the side: $SHLD at 0.92%.
  • You want real Kratos exposure on purpose: buy $KTOS directly and size it like a single stock, not a fund. None of these ETFs gives you more than about 5%.

My Take

I'm a dividend investor first. I reached financial freedom by building income, not by guessing which defense contractor wins the next drone program. But Kratos is a good example of why I always look inside a fund before buying it. Most people would assume a "space ETF" owns the satellite ground system makers. For Kratos, the real exposure is in two ARK funds and a defense ETF.

The company's orders and backlog look like a business that's growing fast. The stock looks like one investors have lost patience with. Both can be true for a while. If you own $ARKX or $ARKQ, you already own about 5% Kratos, so I wouldn't stack a big direct position on top of that without knowing it. And keep any single stock like this to a size you can hold through another 50% drop, because it just did that.
