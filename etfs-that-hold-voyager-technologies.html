Which ETFs Hold Voyager Technologies ($VOYG) Stock? The Full List for 2026

Four funds hold Voyager, the biggest position is about 3%, and the largest space ETFs skip it. Weights, fees, what the Starlab company actually does, and why no fund gives you much of it.

From Our Network
Quick answer: The ETFs with the most Voyager Technologies ($VOYG) are $NASA (Tema Space Innovators ETF) at about 3.1%, $WARP (VanEck Space ETF) at about 2.6%, $ROKT (SPDR S&P Kensho Final Frontiers ETF) at about 2.4% and $MARS (Roundhill Space & Technology ETF) at about 1.8%. $UFO, $ARKX and $ORBX don't show it in their top 25. Weights are as of November 12 to 16, 2026 and change daily.

My lists of ETFs that hold Rocket Lab, Redwire and Intuitive Machines are some of the most read pages on this site. Voyager is the next name people ask me about, and it's a different kind of space stock.

It's not a rocket company and it's not a satellite phone company. Voyager sells defense hardware, builds space station parts, and is working on Starlab, a commercial space station meant to keep people in orbit after the International Space Station retires. That mix is why it lands in both pure space funds and the space plus defense fund $ROKT.

$VOYG closed at $27.04 on November 13, 2026, a market value of about $1.7 billion. It went public on the NYSE on June 11, 2025 at $31.00 a share, so it's trading below its IPO price. Here's every way to own it in a fund, and how little of it you actually get.

The Four Funds, One by One

$NASA Tema Space Innovators ETF

0.75% expense ~$945M AUM $VOYG 3.10%

The most Voyager of any ETF I found, 3.10%, and its 10th biggest holding. $NASA is also the biggest of the new space funds by far, with about $945 million since it launched on March 30, 2026. Its top three are a SpaceX SPV position (16.39%), Rocket Lab (11.01%) and listed SpaceX shares (7.67%). It also holds Firefly Aerospace (4.68%), Intuitive Machines (3.53%) and BlackSky (2.74%), so it's the most "small space company" heavy fund on this list.

$WARP VanEck Space ETF

0.50% expense ~$41M AUM $VOYG 2.56%

Voyager is 2.56% (#17). $WARP is the most concentrated fund here, with only about 22 holdings, and SpaceX (20.66%) plus Rocket Lab (14.05%) make up over a third of it. Launched May 6, 2026. If you like a tight list at a lower fee, this is the one, but understand the Voyager slice is a rounding error next to those two.

$ROKT SPDR S&P Kensho Final Frontiers ETF

0.45% expense ~$146M AUM $VOYG 2.35%

This is the odd one out, and the part most people miss. $ROKT doesn't hold SpaceX in its top 25 at all. It's a space and deep sea exploration fund, so its top holdings are Forum Energy Technologies (4.36%), Lockheed Martin (4.10%) and RTX (4.02%). It spreads money fairly evenly, which is why Voyager at 2.35% is only its 25th largest position. It's the cheapest fund on the list and the oldest, launched October 22, 2018. Want Voyager without a giant SpaceX bet on top? $ROKT is the only option.

$MARS Roundhill Space & Technology ETF

0.75% expense ~$41M AUM $VOYG 1.82%

Voyager is 1.82% (#18). $MARS is the most SpaceX-heavy fund I track at 25.24%, followed by Rocket Lab (9.97%) and Globalstar (5.92%). Launched March 5, 2026. You'd buy $MARS for SpaceX and satellite connectivity, not for Voyager.

What You're Actually Buying With Voyager

Voyager Technologies is based in Denver, was founded in 2019, and changed its name from Voyager Space Holdings in February 2025. It reports three segments:

  • Defense & National Security: solid rocket propulsion parts, signal intelligence systems, laser and radio communications hardware for space, guidance and navigation sensors like star trackers, and AI edge computing.
  • Space Solutions: in-space propulsion, space infrastructure like the Bishop Airlock attached to the ISS, and science and mission management services.
  • Starlab Space Stations: the commercial space station business, built to keep a permanent human presence in low Earth orbit.

Here's how I think about it. The defense business is the part that brings in revenue today. Starlab is the long-dated bet that gets the headlines. When you buy $VOYG you're buying both at once, and the market prices them together.

Revenue is growing, but the company is still losing more than it makes. That's normal for a company building a space station. It also means the stock trades on contracts, milestones and funding news, not on earnings.

The Risks I'd Think About

  • Volatility: a 52-week range of $17.41 to $52.40 means the stock has been roughly cut in half and tripled within the same year. Small space stocks move like this all the time.
  • Losses: a net loss of about $116 million in 2025. Money-losing companies can need more capital, and new shares dilute existing holders.
  • Starlab timing: commercial space stations are huge, multi-year projects that depend on government demand, partners and launch schedules. Delays are the norm in this industry, not the exception.
  • Government customers: a lot of the business depends on defense and NASA budgets, which can shift with politics.
  • Fund concentration: the ETFs that hold Voyager are mostly bets on SpaceX and Rocket Lab. If those two fall, Voyager's small slice won't save you.

Does Voyager Pay a Dividend?

No. Voyager doesn't pay a dividend as of November 2026, and none of the four ETFs above is built for income. That's the honest truth about the whole space theme: it's a growth bet. If you want to see how space funds stack up once everything is counted, check ETFTotalReturns.com.

Which Approach Fits You

  • You want the most Voyager in one fund: $NASA at 3.10%, plus the biggest asset base of the new space funds.
  • You want Voyager without a big SpaceX bet: $ROKT, at 0.45%, the cheapest on the list.
  • You want a tight, cheaper space basket: $WARP at 0.50%, but it's mostly SpaceX and Rocket Lab.
  • You want SpaceX first and a little Voyager on the side: $MARS. See my full list of ETFs that hold SpaceX.
  • You want real Voyager exposure on purpose: no ETF gives you much. That means buying the stock, and sizing it like a small speculative position.

My Take

I'm a dividend investor first. I reached financial freedom by building income, not by guessing which space station gets built first. But Voyager is a good reminder of why I always look inside a fund before I buy it.

If you bought a space ETF because you liked the Starlab story, you own very little of it. You mostly own SpaceX and Rocket Lab. That's fine, just own it on purpose and keep it to a size you can sit through a 50% drop with.
