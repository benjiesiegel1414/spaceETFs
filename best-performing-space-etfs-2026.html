Best Performing Space ETFs in 2026: Every Fund Ranked by Return

I ranked every space and aerospace ETF by 1 year, year to date and since inception return. $ROKT leads at +20.0%, most of the field is red, and every 2026 launch is below its starting price. Here's the full scoreboard and what's behind it.

From Our Network
Quick answer: The best performing space ETF over the past year is $ROKT (SPDR S&P Kensho Final Frontiers ETF), up +20.0% through November 20, 2026. Only 3 of the 11 non-leveraged space and aerospace ETFs with a full year of history are in the green: $ROKT (+20.0%), $UFO (+10.2%), $UFOX (+6.6%). Every space ETF that launched in 2026 is below its starting price, with the best of them, $GALX, at -22.1% since launch.

I get asked the same question about space funds almost every week: which one is actually making people money? It's a fair thing to ask. There are now 33 space, aerospace and SpaceX funds on my live space ETF rankings, and more than half of them did not exist a year ago.

So I pulled the returns for every one of them and ranked them. I split the list into three groups, because lumping a 20 year old defense fund in with a fund that launched in July would tell you nothing. First the funds with a full year behind them, then the class of 2026, then the long run numbers. I left the 2x leveraged and inverse funds out of the rankings on purpose, and I'll explain why near the end.

One warning before the numbers. It has been a rough stretch. On November 20 alone most of these funds fell between 3% and 9%, so every figure below looks worse than it would have a week earlier. I'm showing you the numbers as they are, not picking a flattering date.

How I Measured Performance

Three rules, so you can check my work.

One, total return where I can get it. The 1 year and year to date numbers are total returns, meaning price change plus any dividends paid. That matters less for space funds than it does for the income funds I usually write about, because most space ETFs pay little or nothing. But it's the honest way to compare.

Two, the same end date for everybody. Every number here is through the market close on Friday, November 20, 2026.

Three, new funds get their own table. A fund that launched in May cannot have a 1 year return. For those I use the price return from the fund's first closing price. That's a clean measure of what an investor who bought on day one has experienced, but it is not comparable across funds that launched in different months.

The data comes from StockAnalysis.com fund pages and price histories, which is the same source that feeds the returns columns on my rankings table. That table updates on its own, so by the time you read this the live numbers will have moved. Use this post for the story and the table for today's figures.

Why the Newest Funds Are Down the Most

Two things happened at once, and both hurt the new funds more than the old ones.

First, timing. Most of these funds launched into the excitement around the SpaceX IPO. SpaceX started trading on June 12, 2026 and closed its first day at $160.95. Four days later it closed at $201.80, which is still its highest close. Since then it has given back a lot. It closed at $127.79 on November 20, which is -20.6% from that first day close and about 37% below the peak.

Second, what they own. The new funds were built to hold SpaceX, and many of them hold a lot of it. When I checked holdings for my list of ETFs that hold SpaceX, several of the new space funds had it as their largest position by a wide margin. A fund with a fifth of its money in one stock is going to follow that stock.

The chart shows it clearly. Since the IPO, SpaceX is -20.6%, $UFO is -32.2% and $NASA is -46.6%. The funds fell too, and in this stretch they actually fell more than SpaceX did, because the smaller space stocks they also hold were hit even harder than SpaceX was. You can see it in the single stock funds tied to Rocket Lab, AST SpaceMobile and Intuitive Machines, which are all down sharply this year. Diversification protects you from one company blowing up. It does not protect you when the whole theme sells off together.

That's the main thing I'd want a new space investor to understand. These funds are not a way to avoid volatility. They're a way to own the theme without having to pick the winner.

What About the Leveraged Space ETFs?

I track 11 leveraged and inverse space funds on the site, and I left all of them out of the rankings above. They'd fill the bottom of every table and tell you nothing useful about space investing.

Here's the short version. The four funds that aim for 2x the daily move of SpaceX are down between -67.5% and -68.7% since mid June, while SpaceX itself fell about a third over the same period. That's about double the loss, and a little worse, because these funds reset every day and a choppy stock grinds them down. The single stock funds tied to AST SpaceMobile and Intuitive Machines are down more than 90%.

They're trading tools for people who watch them daily. I wrote a full breakdown in Leveraged SpaceX ETFs Explained, including why the inverse funds barely made money even though SpaceX dropped.

How I'd Use These Rankings

I'm a dividend investor first, so I look at a table like this differently than a trader would. A few things I'd keep in mind.

Last year's winner is not a buy signal. $ROKT won this year partly because it owned less of what fell. Next year the crowded names could lead again and the order could flip. I'd rather understand why a fund did what it did than chase the top row.

Check the overlap before you own two. A lot of these funds hold the same 15 or 20 stocks in different amounts. Owning $NASA, $ORBX and $WARP together mostly gets you the same companies three times with three fees.

Fees are the one number you control. Returns bounce around. The expense ratio comes out every year no matter what. The cheapest space funds charge 0.30% to 0.50% and the most expensive charge 0.99%. I put every fund's fee side by side in Space ETF Fees Compared.

Size it like a side bet. A theme that can drop 30% or 40% in five months belongs in the part of your portfolio you can leave alone. If you're new to these, start with Space ETFs 101.

What You're Actually Buying With Kratos

Kratos is based in San Diego, has about 4,300 employees and reports two segments. Here's what each brought in during the third quarter of 2026 (the quarter ended September 27, 2026):

  • Kratos Government Solutions, $397.2 million (up 52.5%, 35.8% organic): the big engine. This includes its space and satellite business (virtualized ground systems that command, track and control satellites), microwave electronics, turbine engines, defense rocket systems and hypersonics, plus training and cyber. The fastest organic growth was in Defense Rocket Systems at 149.0%. Space, Training and Cyber grew 11.6% organically.
  • Kratos Unmanned Systems, $74.6 million (down from $87.2 million): jet powered drones like the Valkyrie and target drones. Kratos says last year's number included a $16.1 million Valkyrie shipment to an international customer.

The space piece matters for a site like this one. Kratos says its Space and Satellite business has a significant new opportunity pipeline, and that its bid and proposal pipeline rose to $15.5 billion, with the most notable increases in space, training and hypersonics. On November 12, 2026 it also announced a portable terminal for space domain awareness under its Knownspace line.

Here's how I read it. The business numbers are strong: revenue up 35.7%, a record $2.65 billion backlog, more than twice as many new orders as revenue last quarter, and a 2027 forecast that calls for roughly 18% to 22% more growth. But the stock is down about 62% in a year anyway. That's the gap you have to be comfortable with. The company is still burning cash as it builds factories and inventory for drones, engines and hypersonics, it barely breaks even on a GAAP basis, and the share count is up about 13% in a year.

The Risks I'd Think About

  • Volatility: a 52-week range of $26.60 to $134.00. It fell 9.31% on November 20, 2026 alone.
  • Government funding: almost all of its revenue depends on US and allied defense budgets. Kratos itself says US defense funding is running under an extended Continuing Resolution, and it expects Q1 2027 to be its lightest quarter because of it.
  • Cash burn: free cash flow was negative $29.4 million in the quarter, and Kratos expects heavy spending on production capacity to keep weighing on cash flow.
  • Thin profits: a GAAP net loss of $0.2 million on $471.8 million of revenue. Adjusted numbers look better, but they leave out stock compensation and amortization.
  • Dilution: shares outstanding rose about 13.4% over the past year.
  • Fund concentration: even in $ARKX, Kratos is about 5%. If SpaceX, Rocket Lab or Tesla fall, that will move these funds a lot more than Kratos will.

Does Kratos Pay a Dividend?

No. Kratos doesn't pay a dividend as of November 2026, and none of the four ETFs above is built for income. That's the trade-off with the whole space and defense tech theme: you're betting on growth and accepting big swings. Which Approach Fits You

  • You want the most Kratos in one fund: $ARKX at 5.16%, with SpaceX and Rocket Lab as the anchors.
  • You want Kratos inside a robotics and autonomy fund: $ARKQ at 4.85%, where it's the third biggest holding.
  • You want the lowest fee and broad defense exposure: $XAR at 2.05%, with a 0.35% expense ratio.
  • You want the defense giants with a little Kratos on the side: $SHLD at 0.92%.
  • You want real Kratos exposure on purpose: buy $KTOS directly and size it like a single stock, not a fund. None of these ETFs gives you more than about 5%.

My Take

I'm a dividend investor first. I reached financial freedom by building income, not by guessing which defense contractor wins the next drone program. But Kratos is a good example of why I always look inside a fund before buying it. Most people would assume a "space ETF" owns the satellite ground system makers. For Kratos, the real exposure is in two ARK funds and a defense ETF.

The company's orders and backlog look like a business that's growing fast. The stock looks like one investors have lost patience with. Both can be true for a while. If you own $ARKX or $ARKQ, you already own about 5% Kratos, so I wouldn't stack a big direct position on top of that without knowing it. And keep any single stock like this to a size you can hold through another 50% drop, because it just did that.
