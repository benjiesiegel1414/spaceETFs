Best Satellite ETFs in 2026: Which Space Funds Own the Most Satellite Stocks?

There is no big "satellite ETF", but four space funds put 40% or more of their money into satellite operators and makers before you even count SpaceX and Starlink. I ranked nine funds by how much satellite you actually get, with the weights, fees and the risks.

From Our Network
Quick answer: The ETFs with the most satellite stocks are $WARP (VanEck Space ETF) at about 45%, $MARS (Roundhill Space & Technology ETF) at about 42%, $ORBX (Global X Space Tech ETF) at about 41% and $DIPR (Corgi Space & Satellite Communications ETF) at about 36%, counting satellite operators, Earth imaging companies and satellite makers like Iridium, Globalstar, Viasat, EchoStar, AST SpaceMobile, MDA Space and Planet Labs. Add SpaceX, which runs Starlink, and $MARS, $WARP and $ORBX are all around two thirds satellite and Starlink. Holdings as of November 20 to 24, 2026.

My lists of ETFs that hold Iridium, AST SpaceMobile and SpaceX keep pulling in people who search for one thing: a satellite ETF. The problem is that there isn't really one. There's no fund called "the satellite ETF" with billions in it. What there is, is a group of space funds where satellites quietly make up most of the money.

So I did the math. I took the top holdings of every major space fund and added up how much of each one sits in satellite companies. Here's what you actually get.

Here's what jumps out. The three newest pure space funds, $WARP, $MARS and $ORBX, are really satellite funds with a SpaceX core. Strip out SpaceX and they still have more than 40% in satellite companies, spread across 11 to 15 names. Put $10,000 into $WARP and about $4,540 lands in satellite operators and makers, plus about $2,230 in SpaceX.

The biggest funds by assets are a different story. $NASA and $UFO hold far more money, but a smaller share of it is in satellite stocks.

What I Counted as a Satellite Stock

This is the part that changes the answer, so here's exactly what's in the "satellite stocks" column:

  • Satellite communications operators: Iridium ($IRDM), Globalstar ($GSAT), Viasat ($VSAT), EchoStar ($ECHO), AST SpaceMobile ($ASTS), SES, Eutelsat, Telesat ($TSAT), SKY Perfect JSAT, Ovzon and Gilat Satellite Networks ($GILT).
  • Earth imaging and satellite data: Planet Labs ($PL), BlackSky ($BKSY), Satellogic ($SATL), Spire Global ($SPIR), HawkEye 360, Synspective, QPS and Satrec Initiative.
  • Satellite makers: MDA Space ($MDA), which builds satellites and space robotics.

What I left out: SpaceX (shown separately), rocket companies like Rocket Lab and Firefly, defense contractors, and companies that use satellites but don't run them, like Garmin, Trimble and Sirius XM. Rocket Lab also builds satellite parts, so if you count it, the satellite share of $WARP, $MARS and $ORBX goes up another 9 to 13 points. I also only counted the top 25 holdings, so funds with long lists may own a little more than shown.

$DIPR Corgi Space & Satellite Communications ETF

0.35% expense ~$1.1M AUM Satellite 35.6%

The only fund with "satellite" in its name, and the cheapest at 0.35%. It's actively managed and holds 30 stocks. The satellite names are Viasat (5.50%), Globalstar (5.42%), Iridium (5.08%), EchoStar (5.04%), MDA Space (4.11%), Telesat, AST SpaceMobile, Planet Labs, BlackSky, Spire and Satellogic. But it also holds Lockheed Martin (9.01%) and Garmin (7.09%), which pulls the pure satellite share down. The bigger problem is size: about $1.1 million in assets and very thin trading. That's closure risk, and it's why I'd treat it as one to watch rather than one to lean on. Launched May 6, 2026. Live data is on the $DIPR fund page.

The oldest pure space fund here (April 11, 2019) and much bigger than the three leaders. About 27% satellite across EchoStar (5.22%), Viasat (4.31%), Globalstar (3.87%), Iridium (3.33%), MDA Space (3.28%), Planet Labs, AST SpaceMobile, SES and SKY Perfect JSAT. It also owns Sirius XM (6.10%), Garmin and Trimble, which depend on satellites without running the classic satellite businesses I counted. Space ETFs With Little or No Satellite Exposure

  • $NASA (Tema Space Innovators ETF): about 21% satellite in its top 25, led by Viasat (4.97%), MDA Space (4.67%) and EchoStar (3.19%). Its bigger bets are SpaceX (about 27% combined), Rocket Lab and space component makers like Universal Microwave Technology and Filtronic. It's the biggest new space fund at about $839 million.
  • $ARKX (ARK Space & Defense Innovation ETF): no satellite operators in the top 25 at all. It's SpaceX, L3Harris, Deere, Amazon, Rocket Lab and drone makers.
  • $ROKT (SPDR S&P Kensho Final Frontiers ETF): Iridium (3.65%) and Viasat (2.69%) and that's it. It's mostly aerospace and defense, plus ocean technology names.
  • $GALX (VistaShares Space Supercycle ETF): about 7% across MDA Space, Gilat and AST SpaceMobile. It leans on space components and defense electronics.
  • Aerospace and defense funds like $ITA, $XAR and $PPA: built around aerospace and defense contractors, not satellite operators.

This is the part most people miss. If you bought $ARKX or $ROKT because they're "space ETFs", you own almost no satellite companies. The fund name doesn't tell you. The holdings do.

The SpaceX and Starlink Question

Here's the honest complication. The biggest satellite business in the world is Starlink, and it's inside SpaceX. So any fund with a big SpaceX position is also a big satellite fund in practice, even if the rest of its list is rockets.

That cuts both ways. In $MARS, $ORBX and $WARP, SpaceX is 22% to 27% of the fund, so how SpaceX trades will drive your result more than Iridium or Globalstar ever will. If you want satellite exposure without one giant position deciding everything, $DIPR and $UFO hold less SpaceX, at about 15%. I covered every way to own it in which ETFs hold SpaceX, and even a few non-space funds own it now. Over on my sister site, the AIQ ETF review at RobotETFs.com shows SpaceX sitting in the top 10 of Global X's big AI fund.

The Risks I'd Think About

  • Small funds: $WARP, $MARS and $ORBX each hold under $40 million, and $DIPR about $1.1 million. Small funds can close, and trading spreads can be wider. Use limit orders.
  • Short track records: every top satellite fund here launched in 2026, so none of them has been through a full bear market.
  • Single stock weight: SpaceX is 15% to 27% of most of these funds. One stock can make or break your year.
  • Capital intensive businesses: satellite companies spend huge amounts building and launching constellations before they earn it back. Several of the smaller ones, like AST SpaceMobile, Planet Labs and BlackSky, are still early in building out their networks, so the swings can be big.
  • Competition: Starlink's scale puts pressure on older satellite operators' pricing, which is a risk for names like Viasat, SES and Eutelsat.
  • Fees: 0.35% to 0.75% a year. On $10,000 that's $35 to $75 a year. My space ETF fee comparison shows what that adds up to over 20 years.

Which Approach Fits You

  • You want the most satellite exposure, cheaply: $WARP, about 45% satellite at 0.50%.
  • You want the widest spread of global satellite operators: $MARS, 15 satellite names including Telesat, SES, Eutelsat and SKY Perfect JSAT.
  • You want satellites plus a lower fee and big AST SpaceMobile weight: $ORBX at 0.50%.
  • You want size, history and liquidity: $UFO, about 27% satellite with $400 million plus in assets since 2019.
  • You want a dedicated satellite fund and accept tiny assets: $DIPR at 0.35%, but watch its size.
  • You want one satellite company on purpose: buy it directly and size it like a single stock. My Viasat, Globalstar and Planet Labs lists show which fund gives you the most of each.

My Take

I'm a dividend investor first. I reached financial freedom by building income, not by betting on which satellite constellation wins. But satellites are one of the parts of the space economy that already make real money, from phone and internet service to Earth imaging, and I like that this theme isn't just about rockets and dreams.

What I'd do is pick the fund whose holdings match what you actually want, and then check how much SpaceX comes with it. For most people that's the real decision. And keep it a small slice. Almost none of these funds pay a meaningful dividend, and most of them are less than a year old. The best performing space ETFs ranking shows how bumpy this year has been.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best satellite ETF?

There is no single best one, but by how much of the fund sits in satellite stocks, the VanEck Space ETF ($WARP) led at about 45% of assets in satellite operators, imaging companies and satellite makers as of November 24, 2026, followed by the Roundhill Space & Technology ETF ($MARS) at about 42% and the Global X Space Tech ETF ($ORBX) at about 41%. All three also hold 22% to 27% in SpaceX, which runs Starlink.

Is there an ETF that only holds satellite stocks?

Not really. The closest by name is the Corgi Space & Satellite Communications ETF ($DIPR), launched May 6, 2026 with a 0.35% expense ratio, but it also holds SpaceX, Lockheed Martin and Garmin, and it had only about $1.1 million in assets in November 2026. Satellite stocks were about 36% of it.

Which ETF has the most Iridium, Globalstar and Viasat?

Among the funds I track, $ORBX had the most Iridium at about 5.50%, $MARS had the most Globalstar at about 6.60%, and $DIPR and $WARP had the most Viasat at about 5.50% and 5.20%, based on StockAnalysis.com holdings data dated November 20 to 24, 2026.

Do space ETFs like ARKX and ROKT hold satellite stocks?

Barely. The ARK Space & Defense Innovation ETF ($ARKX) showed no satellite operators in its top 25 holdings as of November 23, 2026, and the SPDR S&P Kensho Final Frontiers ETF ($ROKT) held only Iridium (about 3.65%) and Viasat (about 2.69%).

Do satellite ETFs pay dividends?

Not much. Most of these funds pay little or nothing, because the satellite companies they own reinvest heavily in building and launching satellites. They are growth funds, not income funds.
