Leveraged SpaceX ETFs Explained: $SPCH vs $SPAX vs $SPAL vs $SPCU (and the Short Funds)

Six ETFs promise 2x the daily move of SpaceX, up or down. From June to November the stock fell 34%, the long funds lost about two thirds, and the short funds made less than 10%. Here's how they work, how they differ and who they're really for.

From Our Network
Quick answer: There are four ETFs that aim for 2x the daily move of SpaceX ($SPCX): $SPCH (Leverage Shares, 0.75%), $SPCU (Defiance, 1.31%), $SPAX (T-REX, 1.50%) and $SPAL (GraniteShares, 1.53%). Two more aim for 2x the opposite: $SSPC (0.75%) and $SNK (2.91%). From June 15 to November 20, 2026, SpaceX fell 33.6%. The long funds lost about 68%. The short funds, which you'd expect to be up big, gained only 7% to 9%.

SpaceX started trading on June 12, 2026, and the leveraged funds showed up almost immediately. By the next trading day there were six ETFs built to do one thing: give you twice the daily move of $SPCX, up or down. Together they hold more than half a billion dollars, and $SPCH alone has $393M, which makes it bigger than most of the regular space ETFs on my rankings.

I don't own any of them, and I'll tell you why at the end. But a lot of people are searching for them and a lot of money is sitting in them, so I wanted to lay out exactly what they are, how they differ, and what actually happened to the people who bought them in June. The results surprised me, and not in the way you'd guess.

That gap is the whole lesson of this post, so let's walk through why.

What "2x Daily" Really Means

The key word in every one of these fund names is daily. Each fund promises to deliver 2x (or -2x) the move of SpaceX stock for a single day. Then it resets and starts over the next morning. It makes no promise about a week, a month or a year.

That sounds like a small detail. It isn't. Here's the simplest example I know.

Say a stock is at $100. On Monday it rises 10% to $110. On Tuesday it falls 9.09% and it's back to $100. Two days, and the stock is exactly flat.

StartMondayTuesdayTwo Day Result
The stock$100.00+10.00% to $110.00-9.09% to $100.000.0%
2x long fund$100.00+20.00% to $120.00-18.18% to $98.18-1.8%
2x short fund$100.00-20.00% to $80.00+18.18% to $94.55-5.5%

A made up example to show the math. It ignores fees and financing costs, which make the real results a little worse.

The stock went nowhere, and both leveraged funds lost money. The long fund lost a little. The short fund lost more. Nobody did anything wrong and nothing broke. That's just what happens when you multiply each day's move and then compound it. People call it volatility decay. The more a stock bounces around, the more it costs you to hold a daily leveraged fund on either side.

And SpaceX bounces around a lot. In its first 112 trading days, the stock moved more than 5% in a single day 24 times. That's roughly one day in five. A 5% day in the stock is a 10% day in these funds.

The SEC has a plain English investor alert on exactly this, and it says these products can perform very differently from what you'd expect if you hold them longer than a day. It's worth five minutes: Leveraged and Inverse ETFs: Specialized Products with Extra Risks for Buy-and-Hold Investors.

What Happened From June to November

Now the real thing. Here's SpaceX next to one long fund ($SPCH) and one short fund ($SNK), all starting at 100 on June 15.

You can split those five months into three acts.

Act one, the slide. SpaceX closed at $192.50 on June 15 and $108.37 on July 31, a drop of about 44%. This was the short funds' moment. $SNK went from $15.25 to $38.30, a gain of about 151%. A steady move in one direction is the best case for a daily leveraged fund, and it paid much more than 2x. Meanwhile $SPCH fell to $5.66 by August 5, down about 73%.

Act two, the rally. From early August to October 6, SpaceX climbed from $108.27 back to $171.92. That's a 59% bounce, but the stock was still about 11% below where it started in June. Here's the ugly part. $SNK gave back everything and more. It fell to $10.79, which was 29% below its June 15 price. Think about that. SpaceX was lower than in June, and the fund that bets against SpaceX was also lower than in June. And $SPCH? It had bounced to $12.91, still down 38% with the stock down only 11%.

Act three, another leg down. SpaceX slid again to $127.79 by November 20. $SNK recovered to $16.36, leaving it up 7% for the whole period, and $SPCH dropped to $6.54, down 69%.

Why the Short Funds Barely Made Money

This is the part most people get wrong, so I'll say it plainly. Being right about the direction was not enough. Someone who bought $SNK on June 15 because they thought SpaceX was overpriced was correct. The stock fell by a third. And they made about 7%.

The reason is the round trip in the middle. When SpaceX fell 44%, $SNK more than doubled. But then the fund was resetting each day from a much bigger number while the stock rallied 59% off a much smaller one. A 2x short fund facing a rally like that loses most of its value. It did. By the time SpaceX rolled over again, $SNK was climbing back from a deep hole.

The long funds went through the same thing in reverse, but the path happened to be kinder to them. They ended close to double the stock's loss.

So here's how I'd sum up five months of real data:

  • If the stock moves steadily one way, the right fund can beat 2x. $SNK did in June and July.
  • If the stock whips back and forth, both sides bleed. That was August through November.
  • Timing matters more than direction. $SNK was up about 151% at its peak and down 29% at its low, in the same five months.

One note on fees. The expense ratio is only part of what these funds cost. They get their leverage through swaps and options, and the financing built into those shows up in performance, not in the headline fee. So I wouldn't pick one of these on expense ratio alone. For a trader, daily volume and the bid/ask spread probably matter more.

Who These Are Actually For

I think these funds are built for one kind of person: a trader with a view on SpaceX for the next day or the next few days, who watches the position closely and has an exit planned before they get in. For that person, a 2x fund is simpler than borrowing on margin or trading options, and you can't lose more than you put in.

They're a bad fit for almost everything else people try to use them for.

  • "I believe in SpaceX long term, so I'll hold the 2x." The fund doesn't promise 2x over the long term. Five months in, the long funds have lost two thirds of their value on a one third drop, and they'd need to triple just to get back to even.
  • "I'll hedge my space ETFs with a short fund." The short funds made 7% to 9% while SpaceX fell 34% and funds like $NASA fell more. That's not much of a hedge.
  • "It's cheap, the fee is under 1%." The fee is the smallest cost here. The decay is the big one.

If what you really want is SpaceX in your portfolio, you have calmer options. You can own the stock. Or you can own a regular space ETF that holds it at a 10% to 25% weight alongside 20 to 60 other companies. I put the full list in Which ETFs Hold SpaceX, and there's a broader map in 13 space ETFs to look at instead of $SPCX.

My Take

I'm a dividend investor. I reached financial freedom by owning things that pay me and leaving them alone, which is about as far from a daily reset 2x fund as you can get. So take my bias into account.

But I don't think these funds are a scam, and I don't think the people who run them are hiding anything. Every one of them says "daily" right in the name and explains the risk in the prospectus. The problem is that most of us read "2x SpaceX" and stop reading. The June to November numbers are the best explanation I've seen of what the fine print means: the stock fell a third, the longs fell two thirds, and the shorts went almost nowhere.

If you trade them, trade them small and know your exit. If you want to invest in the space economy, there are 14 non-leveraged space funds on my rankings that will let you sleep a lot better. I ranked them by return in Best Performing Space ETFs in 2026.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a 2x SpaceX ETF?

Yes, four of them. $SPCH (Leverage Shares), $SPCU (Defiance), $SPAX (T-REX) and $SPAL (GraniteShares) each aim for 2x the daily return of SpaceX stock ($SPCX). $SPCH is the largest at $393M in assets as of November 2026.

Is there an inverse or short SpaceX ETF?

Yes. $SSPC (Leverage Shares 2X Short SPCX Daily ETF) and $SNK (GraniteShares 2x Short SpaceX Daily ETF) both aim for 2x the opposite of SpaceX's daily move. From June 15 to November 20, 2026 they returned +9.0% and +7.3% while SpaceX fell 33.6%.

Can I hold a leveraged SpaceX ETF long term?

You can, but the funds only target 2x for a single day and then reset. Over longer periods the result depends on the path the stock takes, and choppy trading wears down both long and short funds. The SEC warns that these products can perform very differently from what buy and hold investors expect.

Which 2x SpaceX ETF has the lowest fee?

$SPCH lists the lowest expense ratio of the four long funds at 0.75%, compared with 1.31% for $SPCU, 1.50% for $SPAX and 1.53% for $SPAL. The expense ratio does not include the financing costs built into the swaps and options these funds use.

How much have the 2x SpaceX ETFs lost?

From their June 15, 2026 closing prices to November 20, 2026, the four 2x long SpaceX ETFs lost between 67.5% and 68.7%. SpaceX stock fell 33.6% over the same dates.

What is volatility decay?

It is the drag that comes from multiplying each day's return and compounding it. If a stock rises 10% and then falls back to where it started, a 2x long fund ends down about 1.8% and a 2x short fund ends down about 5.5%. The more the stock swings, the bigger the drag.

What You're Actually Buying With Kratos

Kratos is based in San Diego, has about 4,300 employees and reports two segments. Here's what each brought in during the third quarter of 2026 (the quarter ended September 27, 2026):

  • Kratos Government Solutions, $397.2 million (up 52.5%, 35.8% organic): the big engine. This includes its space and satellite business (virtualized ground systems that command, track and control satellites), microwave electronics, turbine engines, defense rocket systems and hypersonics, plus training and cyber. The fastest organic growth was in Defense Rocket Systems at 149.0%. Space, Training and Cyber grew 11.6% organically.
  • Kratos Unmanned Systems, $74.6 million (down from $87.2 million): jet powered drones like the Valkyrie and target drones. Kratos says last year's number included a $16.1 million Valkyrie shipment to an international customer.

The space piece matters for a site like this one. Kratos says its Space and Satellite business has a significant new opportunity pipeline, and that its bid and proposal pipeline rose to $15.5 billion, with the most notable increases in space, training and hypersonics. On November 12, 2026 it also announced a portable terminal for space domain awareness under its Knownspace line.

Here's how I read it. The business numbers are strong: revenue up 35.7%, a record $2.65 billion backlog, more than twice as many new orders as revenue last quarter, and a 2027 forecast that calls for roughly 18% to 22% more growth. But the stock is down about 62% in a year anyway. That's the gap you have to be comfortable with. The company is still burning cash as it builds factories and inventory for drones, engines and hypersonics, it barely breaks even on a GAAP basis, and the share count is up about 13% in a year.

The Risks I'd Think About

  • Volatility: a 52-week range of $26.60 to $134.00. It fell 9.31% on November 20, 2026 alone.
  • Government funding: almost all of its revenue depends on US and allied defense budgets. Kratos itself says US defense funding is running under an extended Continuing Resolution, and it expects Q1 2027 to be its lightest quarter because of it.
  • Cash burn: free cash flow was negative $29.4 million in the quarter, and Kratos expects heavy spending on production capacity to keep weighing on cash flow.
  • Thin profits: a GAAP net loss of $0.2 million on $471.8 million of revenue. Adjusted numbers look better, but they leave out stock compensation and amortization.
  • Dilution: shares outstanding rose about 13.4% over the past year.
  • Fund concentration: even in $ARKX, Kratos is about 5%. If SpaceX, Rocket Lab or Tesla fall, that will move these funds a lot more than Kratos will.

Does Kratos Pay a Dividend?

No. Kratos doesn't pay a dividend as of November 2026, and none of the four ETFs above is built for income.   • You want Kratos inside a robotics and autonomy fund: $ARKQ at 4.85%, where it's the third biggest holding.
  • You want the lowest fee and broad defense exposure: $XAR at 2.05%, with a 0.35% expense ratio.

My Take

I'm a dividend investor first. I reached financial freedom by building income, not by guessing which defense contractor wins the next drone program. But Kratos is a good example of why I always look inside a fund before buying it. Most people would assume a "space ETF" owns the satellite ground system makers. For Kratos, the real exposure is in two ARK funds and a defense ETF.

The company's orders and backlog look like a business that's growing fast. The stock looks like one investors have lost patience with. Both can be true for a while. If you own $ARKX or $ARKQ, you already own about 5% Kratos, so I wouldn't stack a big direct position on top of that without knowing it. And keep any single stock like this to a size you can hold through another 50% drop, because it just did that.
